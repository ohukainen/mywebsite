Using AI as a Junior Software Engineer

Published February 27, 2026
AI Tools Software Engineering Workflow

Before my time at Linköping University, I had never come into contact with programming. Something I regret greatly in hindsight, maybe in a similar way that I suspect athletes can feel about not finding their sport earlier. But ever since that first course in Python, I was hooked, and programming became one of my biggest hobbies. Today, a little less than five years later, I’m writing this post after my first six months at Sectra as a full time Software Engineer.

When I took that first course in Python, people were just starting to talk about this thing called ChatGPT. Something that feels crazy to think about considering where we are today. Discovering programming at this point in time meant that for at least the first three years of learning how to program, I almost didn’t use any AI tools. This is a stark contrast to the environment in which I have been, and am now, learning .NET and C#, an unexplored technology for me prior to working at Sectra. Now, as compared to just three or four years ago, AI has gotten a lot better, and how it is integrated into the everyday workflows of engineers has also improved greatly. After a couple of weeks at my current job, I was given the opportunity to use Claude Code for the first time, and to be honest, it felt amazing. It could locate problematic code in the huge unfamiliar code-base, produce reasonable fixes, and best of all, I really did feel productive. However, sitting here now, I realize that the way that I have come to use, and in some ways put pressure on myself to use, AI tools has been to the detriment of my own learning.

That raises the question, how do I go forward towards fixing this? This question has been simmering in the back of my mind for some weeks now and I have been able to boil down the answer to a set of rules that I will be enforcing on myself from now on, they are as follows:

Allowed AI usage is inversely proportional to my skill level in the area of application. The first and maybe most obvious takeaway of this rule is: if I don’t feel comfortable with the area I’m working in, I won’t use AI to produce any code. As my knowledge of a language, package, or part of the code-base grows so does my ability to offload work to AI. It is however important that when I use AI, I do it in a way that facilitates learning. A recent study by Anthropic identified ways of using AI that both hurt and help learning. It concluded that letting AI write code that solves the issue you are working on, and relying on AI to debug that produced code, hurt skill acquisition. In contrast, the article found that asking conceptual questions regarding the task, accompanying code queries with requests for explanations, and that manually copying and pasting generated code into the code-base, led to skill acquisition similar to that of people not using AI.

Usage of AI is allowed for technologies and tasks I don’t want/need to be an expert in. This means that the first rule can be circumvented if I work in an area I don’t need or want to become an expert in. For me, a good example of such an area is web development (mainly CSS). I don’t need or want to know this technology well, and luckily, AI is pretty good at it. This website has for example to a great extent been generated with AI, not including the content, of course. This is a highly personal rule and it can be hard to know what technologies are safe to not learn. A question that helped me define what technologies I don’t need to learn is: Am I fine with still being a novice in this area ten years from now? If the answer to that question is yes, AI is allowed.

Don’t be afraid to be junior. As the title of this post suggests, I am a junior software engineer, and the only thing this means is that I have not had as much time as my more senior peers to learn about what I will hopefully be doing for (at least) the next 40 years. This also puts me in a unique position, where not knowing things is more tolerated. Therefore, telling your team that a task might be taking a bit longer, because you want to spend some time really understanding it and the topics around it, should be seen as a good thing (taking into account shared deadlines of course). This rule is probably the most difficult to practice, but letting the allure of early productivity win over the desire to outgrow my current junior level will only hurt me in the long run. For me, following this rule becomes easier when thinking of it as “taking time to really learn” rather than “refusing AI tools”, even when the former can’t be achieved without the latter.

I don’t know what working as a Software Engineer will look like in thirty years, and if it turns out as different from today as today is from thirty years ago, I don’t even dare guess. However, something I do know is that as long as junior engineering positions are necessary, they will in part be created to facilitate learning, so I encourage my junior peers to dare to walk the harder path. This way I hope that we will, in ten or twenty years from now, not be juniors whose growth has been limited to the improvements of AI, but rather seniors having AI as just another tool.
